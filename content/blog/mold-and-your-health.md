Walk through the mold-information landscape online and you'll find two extremes: wellness sites claiming mold is responsible for nearly every chronic illness, and minimizers insisting that "a little mold never hurt anyone." The truth is somewhere in between — and the real story is more nuanced and more useful than either camp admits.

Here's what the peer-reviewed science actually tells us, and what it means for how you should respond to mold in your home.

## Common Symptoms of Mold Exposure

The most well-established health effects of indoor mold exposure affect the respiratory system, skin, and eyes. These include:

- **Allergic rhinitis** — sneezing, runny nose, congestion
- **Itchy, watery, or red eyes**
- **Skin rashes or eczema flare-ups**
- **Asthma exacerbation** — one of the best-documented relationships
- **Cough, wheezing, chest tightness**
- **Recurrent sinus infections**
- **Throat irritation**

These are the mainstream, non-controversial effects, confirmed by WHO, CDC, EPA, and major allergy/immunology societies.

## Symptoms With Emerging or Mixed Evidence

A second tier of symptoms shows up repeatedly in case series and observational studies, though the evidence base is still developing:

- **Headaches** (particularly morning headaches)
- **Brain fog and difficulty concentrating**
- **Fatigue**
- **Sleep disturbance**
- **Joint pain**
- **Mood changes, including anxiety or depression**

Many of these are reported consistently by patients with confirmed high-exposure environments and subside when exposure is removed. The mechanism remains debated — whether mycotoxins, VOCs, or immune system dysregulation — but the pattern is real enough that most physicians now include environmental mold exposure in their differential for unexplained chronic symptoms.

## Who's Most Vulnerable

Mold affects everyone, but some people face substantially higher risk:

- **Children** (developing respiratory and immune systems)
- **Older adults** (reduced respiratory resilience)
- **People with asthma** (vastly more likely to have asthma attacks)
- **People with allergies** (existing hyperreactivity)
- **Immunocompromised individuals** (chemotherapy, organ transplant, HIV) — mold can cause opportunistic infections in this group
- **Pregnant women** (emerging evidence links exposure to respiratory issues in infants)
- **People with CIRS** (Chronic Inflammatory Response Syndrome) — a subset of the population has a genetic HLA variant that predisposes them to severe, systemic reactions

If you or a family member falls into one of these categories, the threshold for action on suspected mold should be lower.

## What "Toxic Black Mold" Actually Does

*Stachybotrys chartarum* — the species that kicked off the modern mold-panic cycle — is serious but often mischaracterized. What the evidence supports:

- Stachybotrys produces mycotoxins called trichothecenes.
- Trichothecenes are toxic at high doses (confirmed in laboratory animal studies at concentrations much higher than typical residential exposure).
- Occupants of severely water-damaged buildings with Stachybotrys contamination do experience elevated rates of respiratory, neurological, and cognitive symptoms.
- Healthy adults with brief, low-level exposure are generally not at risk of the dire outcomes the internet likes to suggest.

The reasonable posture: treat Stachybotrys as a serious remediation priority, especially in homes with children, pregnant women, or immunocompromised occupants — without treating it as a death sentence.

## When to See a Doctor

Talk to a physician — ideally one with experience in environmental medicine — if:

- Your symptoms correlate clearly with time spent in a specific building.
- A family member's chronic symptoms (especially respiratory or neurological) aren't explained by other causes.
- You're immunocompromised and there's a known mold issue in your environment.
- You've confirmed mold remediation is needed and want baseline testing (IgE, IgG to specific species) to track recovery.

Environmental medicine specialists can help interpret test results that general practitioners may be less familiar with.

## What Medical Testing Can (and Can't) Tell You

Useful tests:

- **Specific IgE allergy testing** — confirms allergic sensitivity to specific mold species.
- **Pulmonary function testing** — documents asthma or reduced lung function.
- **ENT/sinus imaging** — shows fungal sinusitis when relevant.

Less reliable (controversial in mainstream medicine):

- **Urine mycotoxin tests** — currently not validated by reputable medical organizations; results should be interpreted cautiously.
- **Blood mycotoxin panels** — similar caveats apply.

This is a fast-moving area. What's fringe today may become mainstream in five years, or may not. For now, documented IgE testing plus environmental sampling of the home is the most defensible combination.
